Method

  1. Data. NetHealth, a University of Notre Dame study that followed about 700 undergraduates from 2015 to 2019 (public, CC BY 4.0). I use the second survey round and the friends each student named in it, keeping pairs where both students took part.
  2. Questions. Five questions where real friends agree far more than chance: beer, wine or liquor, political party, legalising marijuana and same-sex marriage. Wording and options are taken from the study’s questionnaire.
  3. Baseline. Each student is simulated alone from gender, race and home region, the same three facts the Artificial Societies Benchmark uses for this study.
  4. Background and beyond. Shuffle answers among students with the same profile, 2,000 times. Agreement that survives the shuffle is explained by shared background; the rest is beyond it. A simulator that answers one student at a time can only produce the first kind.
  5. Network-aware runs. Each simulated student is also shown what their friends answered in the previous run. No real answers are ever shown to the model.

Limits: one university, one survey round, one small model so far. The network-aware runs test whether showing friends’ answers produces agreement; they do not show that the model has learned how influence works.
